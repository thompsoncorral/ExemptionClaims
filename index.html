What a draftee should know

You cannot be directly drafted into the military. A draft letter is simply an order telling you to show up at MEPS for medical, mental, and exemption evaluation.

Registration is not induction — what the statute actually says

Under 50 U.S.C. § 3803(a), every registrant shall be "immediately liable for classification and examination, and shall, as soon as practicable following his registration, be so classified and examined, both physically and mentally, in order to determine his AVAILABILITY for induction for military training and service in the Armed Forces." Induction is explicitly a separate, determination — induction is not the same event as being called up for evaluation by a draft letter. Showing up for evaluation is what the law requires. What happens afterward is a different legal question.

After December 15, 2026 all young men are no longer liable for "classification" because the selective service will "automatically register" men for the draft process. Therefor all draftees only have one lawful obligation or liability - to show up at MEPS for exemption entitlements and medical examinations - additionally a draftee can send a exemption letter ten days prior to any MEPS evaluation appointment date to defer that evaluation and shift the entire process to the local volunteer appeals board.

YOU ARE NOT LIABLE TO JOIN THE MILITARY - you are liable to "show up" to MEPS for an examination of "availability". Additionally a draftee can send meps an exemption letter ten days before your MEPS evaluation date so the selective service appeals board can "determine your exemptions to availability". That's it nothing more! So if you "show up" to examination and verbally claim exemptions at MEPS or you send your official exemption letter up to ten days ahead of time you have fulfilled all your lawfull obligations of the entire selective service process and complied with the law in totality!

What most people call the "college deferment" no longer exists. Before 1971, full-time students got Class II-S — protection that ran until graduation, effectively covering the whole college degree program of the student! A college student was historically deferred from the draft lottery, but not anymore.

Congress ended that in 1971 (via H.R. 6531, signed by Nixon). What replaced it isn't a college deferment at all — it's a semester deferment. Under current Selective Service rules, a student's induction can only be postponed to the end of the current semester (or, for seniors, the end of the academic year) — not until the degree is finished.

That means, for example, if a draftee receives a draft notice in the middle of a school semester — say, around Thanksgiving — the draftee would now be forced to process at MEPS a few weeks later in mid-December at the end of the school semester, regardless of how much of the degree remains. The government still calls it "college deferment," but Americans will be shocked to learn it's only a few weeks' deferment. Score 9th percentile or below on the AFQT, and you're disqualified from military service — no exemption needed.

Many draftees will be denied induction into military service because they achieve a failing score on the ASVAB. If you score in Category V (1st–9th percentile) on the AFQT, you cannot join the military.

"Exemption = a status-based legal carve-out. It exists regardless of your physical condition — it's about who you are (a duly ordained minister, or a conscientious objector). An exemption is something you claim, and a board grants it based on your status/beliefs, not your body.

  • Disqualification = a fitness-based finding. It's about whether your body/mind meets the military's medical, mental, and moral standards. This is what MEPS actually exists to determine. You don't "claim" a disqualification — MEPS tests for it and either finds you fit or unfit.

So where does the ASVAB fit? The ASVAB (Armed Services Vocational Aptitude Battery) is neither an exemption nor strictly a medical disqualification — it's a third, separate category: an aptitude/qualification screen. §3803(a) itself references this directly — it sets "the passing requirement for the Armed Forces Qualification Test... at a percentile score of 10 points." Scoring below that threshold doesn't exempt you (you're not claiming any status) and it isn't a medical disqualification (nothing physically or mentally wrong necessarily) — it's a failure to meet the minimum aptitude standard, which independently bars induction."

1) You Are a Civilian Even If Drafted

Again, being drafted does not place you into the military — this is very important for draftees to understand and remember. You are not drafted into the military; you are drafted to report to MEPS for exemption evaluation as a civilian. This is why your appeal goes before a local volunteer review board — who could even be your own neighbors — and never before a JAG (Judge Advocate General) military court.

Yes, everyone at MEPS remains a civilian throughout the entire evaluation process. The physical exam, mental screening, moral/background review, and ASVAB are all things done to a civilian, not a service member. Nothing about being present at MEPS or being processed changes your legal status.

The exact legal trigger for the status change is the Oath of Enlistment/Induction, codified at 10 U.S.C. § 502, and confirmed directly: "Taking the oath immediately places the individual under the jurisdiction of military law and discipline." Another source pins the statutory basis even more precisely: "Under federal law, taking the oath is the moment your change of status becomes effective and you fall under military jurisdiction (10 U.S.C. § 802 – Art. 2, Persons Subject to This Chapter)."

MEPS Is Regulated to Treat You Appropriately

This isn't just an absence of authority over you — it's an affirmative duty on MEPS itself. USMEPCOM Regulation 601-23 requires that "applicants will always be treated with dignity and respect" under its official Red Carpet Treatment policy. The regulation goes further, instructing staff to recognize that "visiting MEPS is often an applicant's first exposure to the Armed Forces," and that every applicant "has unique concerns, problems, and questions" — deserving of individual attention, not just processing. You're not just protected by the absence of military jurisdiction; the military's own regulations obligate MEPS to treat you well while you're there.

You Maintain All Your Civilian Rights at MEPS

Because you're still a civilian, all your ordinary civilian rights stay in effect — including the right to consent, and the right to withhold it. Much of what MEPS asks for, including your medical history, is explicitly voluntary. The government's own form (DD Form 2807-2) states it plainly: "DISCLOSURE: Voluntary; however, failure to provide the requested information may result in an inability to process your application." A consent form cannot, by definition, be forced — if it could be compelled, it wouldn't be consent.

MEPS staff won't necessarily tell you this out loud. Often they'll simply ask you a battery of medical questions and type your answers directly into a computer — no form in front of you, no reminder that you can decline. That doesn't change your right. You can say "no, thank you" to any question, on paper or spoken aloud, at any point.

MEPS staff have no UCMJ authority over a civilian, no power to send military police after you, and no ability to compel your consent. If you decline, the only real consequence is that your processing doesn't move forward — it gets sent to the appeals board, the same review process that handles every other unresolved claim.

And because you remain a civilian, if MEPS staff cross the line — slander, coercion, threats, or physical mistreatment — you can file charges against them, the same as you could against anyone else, at any time. Even after you receive a draft notice, you remain a civilian throughout the entire process — examination, exemption evaluation, and appeals. If MEPS treats you badly, that means you can file charges against them, sue them, and use what happened as evidence in your own appeal. If MEPS staff treats you badly, they additionally violate their own "red carpet" regulations and internal policy of MEPS staff conduct.

If a draft is ever enacted and you receive an order, here's what the law actually requires of you. You are only "ordered" to show up to be evaluated for availability, exemption, and deferments.

The law does not say you must "join the military." It does not say you must "serve." It says you are liable for classification and examination — meaning you can be called up to be evaluated, not that showing up automatically makes you a soldier. Whether you're ever actually inducted depends entirely on what that evaluation finds, and on whether you have a valid exemption, deferment, or disqualification — none of which can be known until you're actually examined.

Registration itself (§3802) only requires you to give your identity — name, birthdate, address, Social Security number. Nothing more - and that performance clause is void and not applicable after December 2026. The next step, if a draft is activated (§3803), only requires you to show up and be classified and examined. Induction — actually becoming a service member — is a separate, later, conditional outcome that depends on that examination, not something the law requires of you directly.

So when a draft notice arrives, it is not a summons to war. It is a summons to an evaluation. Know that distinction, and you'll understand exactly what the law is actually asking of you — and what it isn't.
