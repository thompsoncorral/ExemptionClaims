Exemptions

The short version

  • Some people are automatically exempt — nothing to do.
  • Others are entitled to an exemption but must claim it, in person at MEPS or by letter.
  • Some people qualify for a deferment.
  • Every decision can be appealed several times before anything else happens.
Automatic

Already exempt — nothing to do

  • Non-immigrant visa holders (foreign students, visitors, tourists, diplomats)
  • Active-duty military personnel
  • Women (currently)
Must claim

Exempt from induction — but you have to claim it

You must still register until December 2026.

  • Duly ordained ministers, clergy / regular ministers (not ordained), and ministerial students
  • Men with disabilities
  • Undocumented immigrants
  • Sole surviving sons
Deferment

Deferments if drafted

  • High school students — until graduation or age 20
  • College students — until the end of the current semester
  • Hardship — induction would cause severe hardship to dependent children
  • Medical or psychological conditions that are disqualifying (determined at MEPS)
  • Certain elected officials — while in office
  • Veterans who already served
  • Conscientious objectors (moral or religious opposition to war) — three ways to qualify:
    1. You're a member of a church with conscientious objector theology
    2. You hold the beliefs yourself
    3. You're the chief duly ordained minister of a church entity with conscientious objector tenets, and you maintain, teach, preach and/or evangelize that faith-based theology

Two ways to claim your exemption

Best strategy Claim it in person at MEPS

Report on your scheduled date and make your claim the moment you arrive. Be courteous — don't waste MEPS staff's time. They forward your case to the local board and send you home.

Skip the trip Send a Letter of Exemption Claim

Postmark it within 10 days of receiving your draft notice. Mail it to your local MEPS, or drop it at the MEPS front desk and get a receipt or signed proof of delivery.

Heads up: local boards are on "standby"

Local boards aren't staffed or operating unless a draft is reactivated, and Selective Service doesn't publish their addresses — so there's often nowhere to mail a letter. Your local MEPS office is active and publicly listed, which is why it's the practical place to send or deliver your claim.

What happens after you claim

  1. You report to MEPS and claim your exemption Your report date is at least 10 days after the order is issued. This fulfills your first "show up" obligation. 32 C.F.R. § 1624.5(a)
  2. MEPS forwards your claim to a volunteer local board 32 C.F.R. § 1605.53
  3. The board schedules your hearing They must give you at least 10 days' notice. Appearing at this hearing fulfills your second "show up" obligation. 32 C.F.R. § 1648.4(a)
  4. Need a later date? You can push it back The board reviews and responds, and you can respond in turn — each exchange adds time before your case is heard. 32 C.F.R. § 1648.4(b)
  5. The board decides your "availability" If your exemption is granted, you're exempt — no further testing needed. If it's denied, you can appeal.
  6. Appeal, appeal, appeal District appeal board→National Appeal Board→The "real" courts Only once your appeals are exhausted do you move toward MEPS evaluation.
This all takes time

At least 20 days are built into the process before your case is even heard the first time. And 10 days is only the required minimum, not a maximum — the actual hearing date depends on the board's own schedule and could land further out.

Why does the letter option exist?

You're entitled by law to show up at MEPS and claim your exemptions in person — that's the sole purpose MEPS exists to serve. The letter isn't a requirement; it's an accommodation, a legal courtesy that lets you skip the trip when your exemption can already be established on paper.

If you did show up on your notice date with a valid exemption, MEPS would send you home anyway — but precious time that could have been used to process other applicants would be wasted.
